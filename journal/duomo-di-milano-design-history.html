Journal · Duomo di Milano

One cathedral, six centuries, three tees that don't agree on how to draw it

Why the Duomo di Milano keeps getting redrawn instead of just getting reprinted.

Most landmarks get one treatment in a catalog and that's the end of it — a single clean illustration, done. The Duomo di Milano has three, across three different Floating Boot collections, and none of them are the same drawing with a different filter. That's not a marketing flex. It's a function of the building itself: the Duomo is so overloaded with detail that there's no single "correct" way to simplify it, which means every artist who takes it on has to make different decisions about what to keep and what to cut. Here's the actual history behind the building, and a close look at what each of the three pieces chose to keep.

A cathedral that took six centuries to finish arguing with itself

Construction started in 1386 under Gian Galeazzo Visconti, Duke of Milan, who wanted a Gothic cathedral to rival anything north of the Alps. It did not go quickly. Architects, popes, dukes, and later Napoleon all had opinions, and the building absorbed centuries of them in layers: the structure went up slowly through the 1400s and 1500s, the facade sat unfinished for generations, and it was Napoleon — crowning himself King of Italy inside the half-built cathedral in 1805 — who ordered the front finally completed, on his own deadline, not the Church's. The last of the bronze doors wasn't installed until 1965. Depending on how you count, the Duomo took somewhere between 580 and nearly 600 years to go from first stone to finished building. Almost nothing else standing in a modern city has a construction timeline that spans that many centuries of changing taste.

Why it's a genuinely hard thing to draw

The Duomo isn't just old, it's dense. It carries around 3,400 statues and 135 spires — more sculptural ornament per square meter than almost any Gothic building in the world — plus the single detail that makes it unmistakably Milanese: the gilded copper statue of the Madonnina, raised onto the tallest spire in 1774, still the symbolic high point of the skyline. The marble itself has a backstory too — it's pale pink-and-white Candoglia marble, quarried in the mountains north of the city and floated down specifically on a canal network partly built to move it, under a ducal decree reserving the stone for the cathedral alone. Every one of those details is a design decision waiting to be made: keep the forest of spires and lose everything else, or keep the Madonnina as the single identifying mark and simplify the rest into a silhouette. There's no neutral way to draw this building — every version is already an argument about what the Duomo "is."

Art Deco Travel — the golden-age poster argument

This is the pick that argues the Duomo is, first, a piece of civic theater: trams crossing the piazza, geometric border, the confident simplified linework of a 1950s ENIT travel poster. It keeps the spires as a clean repeating pattern rather than individually rendered sculpture — the building read as skyline silhouette, not architectural catalog. It's the most "postcard" of the three in spirit, but built the way an actual mid-century illustrator would have built it, not faked with a filter.

Vintage Lithograph — the antique-print argument

This one argues the opposite case: that the Duomo's density is the whole point, and the right move is to lean into the ornament rather than simplify it away. Gold-leaf frame, horse carriages crossing the piazza, the ornate borders of a century-old lithograph — this is the version that treats the building as a thing to be catalogued in fine linework, the way 19th-century print-makers actually drew it, before photography made illustrators start cutting corners.

Ukiyo-e Wave Edition — the technique-crossover argument

The third version doesn't try to simplify or catalogue the Duomo at all — it reframes it, setting the spires rising through a Hokusai-style woodblock wave. The ukiyo-e technique credit is a one-line fact, not the centerpiece: the point isn't "Japan meets Italy" as a novelty, it's that a 19th-century Japanese print technique and a 14th-century Italian Gothic cathedral both reward the same kind of patient, linework-heavy composition, and putting them in the same frame makes that obvious in a way neither building nor wave does alone.
