Four ways to wear Italy, and how not to pick wrong

Vintage Travel, Ukiyo-e Wave, Minimal Line, and Stadi d'Italia aren't four flavors of the same shirt.

At a glance, every collection on this site looks like the same move repeated: take an Italian place, redraw it, put it on a tee. It isn't. Each collection is making a different argument about why a place belongs on your chest in the first place — nostalgia, an unlikely art reference, restraint, or a plain personal memory that has nothing to do with tourism at all. Knowing which argument you actually believe is the fastest way to pick the right one, for yourself or for someone else.

Vintage Travel — for "I want it to look like I meant it"

This collection borrows its whole visual language from a specific, real era: the 1950s and '60s golden age of Italian travel posters, when ENIT and the airlines commissioned actual illustrators to sell Milan, Rome, and the lakes to the world. Warm, slightly muted color, confident simplified linework, sometimes a decorative border or vintage type treatment — the opposite of a photo print. It's the pick for someone who's actually been somewhere and wants an object that reads as kept, not a going-away-gift-shop shirt they'll wear once. Ukiyo-e Wave — for "I like that this shouldn't work, but does"

This one takes Katsushika Hokusai's woodblock tradition — the curling wave, the flattened perspective, the bold negative space that gave us The Great Wave off Kanagawa, and gave Floating Boot its own name — and wraps it around landmarks that have nothing to do with Japan. It's a bolder, more graphic pick than Vintage Travel: less muted nostalgia, more visible crossover, the kind of shirt someone asks you about. Best for people who want the design reference itself to be part of the conversation. See the full collection write-up for how each landmark got reworked.

Minimal Line — for "I don't want it to look like a souvenir at all"

The opposite instinct from the other two: strip the landmark down to one continuous line, no color, no ornament, until it's closer to architectural drafting than illustration. It's the quietest, most wearable pick of the four — the one that works under a jacket, that a stranger has to actually look at twice to clock what it is. Good for someone who wants the Italy reference to be almost private. The Minimal Line piece covers why cutting a landmark down that far is harder than it looks.

Stadi d'Italia — the one that isn't about landmarks at all

This is a different collection entirely, not a fourth variation on the same design problem. There's no illustrator's reinterpretation here — these are a kid's actual stadium drawings, printed as they were, across seventeen Italian grounds from San Siro and the Olimpico down to Cittadella and Cremona. It's not about a place you visited; it's about a building tied to a city, deliberately stripped of team crests and colors so it's not club merch. Right pick if the connection is personal — a specific city, a specific Sunday — rather than "Italy" as a destination. The full story behind the collection explains why it's built the way it is.

So, actually, which one

If you're buying for someone who visited Italy once and wants a keepsake that doesn't look like it came off a folding table: Vintage Travel. If they already own "an Italy shirt" and you want to get them something that reads as a different kind of object entirely: Ukiyo-e Wave if they like a loud design reference, Minimal Line if they'd rather it stay quiet. And if the person in question is actually from somewhere in Italy, or grew up going to a specific ground, skip the landmark tees altogether — Stadi d'Italia is the one that's actually about them, not about Italy as a place other people visit.
